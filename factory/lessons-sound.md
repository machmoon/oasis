- When a brief says something 'dies', 'falls' or 'winds down', make every component follow that arc: pitch and level should sag on a decelerating curve before they stop. Leaving one layer at a constant level and gating it off at the end makes it sound like a switch, not a failure.
- Size the render buffer to the actual envelope (end shortly after the signal falls below threshold), and always apply a short fade on every termination path, including 'tail off', so one-shots neither pad with silence nor cut off mid-tone.
- A switch that removes a tail or release should still apply a short, shaped fade, roughly 30–80 ms scaled to the sound's level at that point. 'No tail' should mean tight, never truncated at full amplitude.
- When one knob multiplies the number of events, scale timing and any continuous bed layer with it, or expose a separate duration/speed knob, so the high end stays discrete events instead of collapsing into a wash.
- When a style choice names a recognisable real-world sound, build in that sound's signature spectral features. For a modem that means tones and frequency stepping, not just gated noise. Otherwise the choice is only a different amplitude envelope over the same texture.
- When a choice knob scales a sound up (bigger, denser, louder), keep the sound's defining feature, like a pitch rise or tonal core, clearly visible above the added layers at every setting. Scale the supporting texture around it, not over it.
- When a brief names two layers (e.g. creak and whir), give each its own temporal signature. Use irregular discrete events for friction and a steady tone for mechanisms, so each reads separately in the spectrogram instead of merging into one band. Also derive total duration from the events actually enabled, so turning off a final event never leaves dead air.
- Every resonator and oscillator must have a decay tied to an envelope that reaches true zero, checked at the minimum of every knob as well as the defaults. A component that only gets damped at certain settings will leave a constant hum under the sound at the extremes.
- When a brief names several layers (e.g. crackle vs debris), give each its own spectral and temporal signature (crackle bright, dense and irregular; debris lower, sparser, with decaying bounce spacing). Then each knob audibly controls a different element, rather than two knobs scaling similar-looking clicks.
- When a brief names a sequence of events (release, motion, impact), give each event its own spectral and envelope identity: a release or click needs a sharp, bright transient, and motion needs continuous sound. Check that each one is visibly distinct in the spectrogram at defaults rather than spending all the detail on the loudest hit.
- When a switch or short setting removes a tail or shortens a sound, end every layer with a short envelope fade (especially low-frequency ones) rather than cutting it off. Make sure the event order that defines the sound (impact, then mechanism, then settle) survives at both range extremes.
- When modelling metallic resonance, build it from many inharmonic, quickly damped partials plus a noise or rattle layer, and jitter their frequencies per hit. Also tie the ring decay to the event interval so tails cannot pile up into a sustained tone at fast rates.
- When a knob adds a named character layer (groan, ring, creak), give it a visibly distinct signature, such as sustained tonal partials with pitch movement. Turning it up should then add a new recognisable element, not just more of the same broadband decay.
