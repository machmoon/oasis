- Design the focal detail for the size it will actually be seen at: check it in the final preview at real resolution and simplify it (fewer, bolder marks; no sub-pixel or sub-voxel detail that aliases into stripes) until it reads instantly. A noisy hero feature sinks an otherwise solid asset.
- When simplifying an object into blocks, keep the parts that define it (wheels on a vehicle, doors on a building) recognisable as those parts at thumbnail size. Check the silhouette before refining livery and colour.
- Every preset must be visibly distinct from the defaults. Any derived colour pair, such as the stripes or highlights generated from a user colour, must be contrast-checked against its own surroundings so a feature never washes out when the base colour changes.
- Map brand colours to roles, not to every surface: reserve the brand's lightest and darkest values for accent parts, and keep focal and emissive elements (lamps, glass, doors) on fixed contrast-safe neutrals so they stay legible whatever the brand pair is.
- Build the element the asset is named after (here the clock face) at the same geometric fidelity as the rest of the model. Check it at thumbnail size in every preset: a stair-stepped circle or illegible glyph on otherwise clean geometry reads as a rendering bug, not a style.
- When a pixel or voxel font is drawn on a grid that scales with a knob, design each glyph at the smallest grid size it will render at and check the word reads correctly at defaults. Don't let glyphs be resampled from a larger master, because snapping drops or adds stray pixels and turns letters into different characters.
- Any rotating or protruding sub-assembly (sails, blades, signs) must be offset from the body along its axis by at least its own thickness plus clearance. Check that offset at every value of its rotation knob so it never interpenetrates other geometry.
- Derive face shading by darkening or multiplying from the input colour rather than lightening toward white, clamp lightness so every face keeps visible contrast against its neighbours and the background, and always test with a very light, very saturated hue (e.g. lime or cyan) as well as a dark one.
- When a secondary surface such as a panel, badge or trim takes its colour from the body colour, derive it with a guaranteed minimum value contrast against the body. Then check it in both the lightest and darkest brand probes, so it never merges into the body.
- When a choice knob swaps in one part, such as a roof, make the new part sit exactly on the same mounting outline as the original, with matching overhang on every side. Then render each option on its own and check that no nearby part pokes through it.
- Before placing props, cover the entire footprint with the ground surface and lay props out against that full plot. Then check the render at a glance for large unintended empty areas or objects standing on bare base. An asset whose ground covers half its footprint reads as unfinished no matter how good the props are.
- Compute face shading as bounded, clamped lightness offsets in a perceptual colour space (e.g. OKLCH ±L with a max-L cap) rather than mixing toward white/black, and test with both very light and very saturated input colours. Otherwise lit faces and derived secondary surfaces wash out and lose the material's identity under arbitrary brand colours.
- Derive attachment points and clearances from the actual dimensions of the parent part, such as the tower axis, cap radius, and door and window positions. Then check every swapped or extreme-valued component against them, so that alternates stay centred and moving parts never cross key features.
- Before submitting, render each range knob at its minimum and maximum and diff against the defaults; any knob that produces no visible change should be fixed or removed, because a dead knob signals an unfinished asset.
- Map brand colours onto accent and structural roles only, never onto materials whose identity depends on their hue (water, foliage, skin, sky). Let brand tint those materials within a bounded hue range instead, so the object still reads as itself under any brand.
- Before shipping, render every range knob at its minimum and its maximum side by side. If the two extremes are not obviously different at thumbnail size, widen the range or cut the knob, because a knob that changes nothing visibly is a broken promise to the buyer.
- Keep small decorative props a clear margin inside the zone they belong to (water, grass, deck) and never centred on a boundary between zones, so they cannot appear to straddle or clip into neighbouring surfaces at any size.
- Build compound props such as a boat with cabin and hull as one group positioned from a shared origin, and constrain it to its allowed region (e.g. inside the water, clear of bank walls). Then check every preset, extreme and brand render to confirm the parts stay attached, stay inside that region, and keep a colour distinct from the surface they sit on.
- Cap the count of repeated fine detail (slats, lattices, bands) as a function of the element's rendered size, so each sub-element stays several pixels wide at every knob extreme instead of collapsing into moiré.
- Give every scattered or count-driven prop an explicit footprint. Clamp placements inside the tile's walkable bounds, inset from the edge, and reject positions that intersect other props or fixed structures (coping, jetty, water vs. land). Then verify at maximum counts, because collisions and edge overhangs only become obvious when density is highest.
- Before adding surface detail, check at thumbnail size that the object still reads as its named material. Generic box-modelling moves like recessed panels and stepped caps make organic subjects look manufactured, so use irregular silhouettes, rounded or lumpy masses and broken edges for anything that grows.
- Make the brief's defining content the visual hero. Give it more volume, colour mass and recognisable form than its containers and supports, and check that the object is identifiable from silhouette alone at the kit's normal viewing distance.
- When mapping brand colours onto a model, derive the structural and secondary parts from the brand colour with an enforced minimum contrast against both the scene background and the neighbouring parts. Then render light and dark brand probes and check that every small feature, such as lamps and trims, is still visible before shipping.
- When several elements derive their colours from the same brand input (e.g. boat and water), enforce a minimum luminance contrast between adjacent elements after derivation, and fall back to a lighter or darker shift when the contrast is too low. Otherwise the focal object vanishes in some brand palettes.
- Lay out every sub-object in a kit piece against reserved footprint cells with clearance, and re-run a collision/bounds check at every count-knob value, so that growing one element (e.g. more swings) can never push it into a neighbour or off the fixed tile footprint.
- Every visible element must be identifiable at a glance and tied to a named knob. Delete decorative bits that cannot be named, and never let a scalar knob like height silently add or remove structures.
- Before shipping, render each option of every choice knob next to the default and diff them; if an option does not produce a clearly visible change at thumbnail size, either make it visibly distinct or remove it.
- Derive every structural member (legs, posts, walkways, lamp arms) from the extents of the main panel, so that supports always sit under the edges they carry and nothing overhangs or floats at any knob value.
- When brand colours drive several roles at once, derive a guaranteed contrast between a figure and the ground it sits on (boat vs water, text vs fill), e.g. by offsetting lightness or using a complementary role. Then verify the figure stays legible in every brand probe, not just the default palette.
- Before shipping a block-kit piece, check every mesh against the briefed footprint and ground plane. Any surface without a clear material role (blank slabs, filler planes, oversized aprons) reads as a broken render, and it also breaks tiling with neighbouring pieces.
- Never put near-letterforms on a hero surface. Either render a real, legible word (or a user-editable text knob), or use clearly abstract graphics, because shapes that almost read as letters look like a typo and undermine the whole asset.
- When a count or size knob makes a part grow, give that part a reserved footprint sized for the knob's maximum, or grow it toward open space. Then render the max before shipping and check that it never intersects neighbouring props.
- When a prop has contents that can be added or moved (buckets, crates, items), keep a clear zone around fixed accessories like lanterns, posts and signs. That way no item count or layout ever lets them overlap or hide each other's silhouettes.
- Before polishing, check that the render reads as the noun in the brief. Edge and surface cues like hard white coping and regular grid lines can flip a natural feature (a pond) into a different object (a pool). If the brief specifies an edge treatment such as grass, build exactly that, and keep geometric patterns off organic surfaces.
- When remapping an asset to an arbitrary brand palette, assign the grounding/structural surfaces (bases, rims, plinths) from the palette's darkest usable tone and enforce a minimum contrast against the canvas, so the silhouette never dissolves into the background regardless of which brand is applied.
- The element that names the asset (here the horses) must be legible at the default camera and thumbnail size, and must not be hidden by other geometry. Scale it, simplify its silhouette and place it where the fixed camera can see it before adding anything else.
- Check small detail elements at the actual delivered render size, in day, night and both brand probes. These include clock faces, glyphs and icons. If they don't read, reduce them to a few bold primitives with fixed contrast against their own backing panel.
- Make the asset's defining sub-element (here the horse) readable by silhouette alone at the final thumbnail size before polishing anything else. Exaggerate its key features (head, neck, ears) over literal voxel accuracy, and make sure any count knob produces a clearly visible change.
- Wherever two parts meet (panel tops under a roof, strips under a fascia), offset or trim one so it never shares a plane with or pokes through the other, then inspect every surface at defaults and at extremes for stray off-colour patches before submitting.
- Every mounted or hanging element must visibly attach to something (bracket, rod or post) and must not intersect neighbouring geometry. Render every curved part at the same smoothness as the rest of the model so no single detail looks stair-stepped or pasted in.
